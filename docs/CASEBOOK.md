# The Casebook
### A guide to writing cases for *Sherlock's Legacy*

Welcome, case-writer.

A case in *Sherlock's Legacy* is a small, self-contained mystery: a handful of rooms, a body, a circle
of suspects, and a trail of clues that - followed carefully - leads to exactly one truth. Done well, it
feels like reading a Golden-Age detective novel where *you* are the detective. Done carelessly, it feels
like a quiz.

This guide walks you through everything you need to write a great one: the rules that keep a mystery
fair, the mechanics the player actually uses, how to design the puzzle backwards from its solution, how
to write suspects and clues that mislead honestly, how to build the final exam, how to give Watson a
voice, how to art-direct the whole thing, and finally what a case file must contain to be valid and
playable.

Throughout, we use one finished case as our running example - **"An Invitation to Judgement"**, a
snowbound medium case - so you can see every principle in a real setting rather than in the abstract.

---

## 1. The shape of a case

Every case is built from the same parts. Learn these names; the rest of the guide uses them constantly.

- **Rooms** - the places the detective can walk between. A small connected map (3 rooms for an easy
  case, up to ~10 for a hard one).
- **Objects (clues)** - things you can *examine*. Each yields a clue, and some double as *evidence*.
- **Suspects** - the people you can *question*. Each hides something; exactly one is the culprit.
- **Deductions** - named conclusions the player earns by *combining* clues or by *contradicting* a
  suspect. The currency of solving.
- **Insight tokens** - a small, limited resource spent to *deduce* and to ask Watson pointed questions.
- **The case file** - the dossier: the victim, an overview, and a profile of each suspect.
- **Watson** - your in-world hint system, with a general nudge, a contradiction nudge, and a paid
  "analyse this person/thing" service.
- **The final exam** - the ending, where the player names the culprit and the truth.

> **Our example.** *An Invitation to Judgement* has 6 rooms (Entrance Hall, Study, Library, Servants
> Hall, Drawing Room, Guest Room), 9 clues, 6 suspects, three combine steps and two contradictions, and
> a three-question final exam. Its host, **Mr. Gray**, is found murdered on the first night of a
> snowbound house party; the truth reaches fifteen years back to the drowning of a governess, **Emma
> Shaw**.

---

## 2. The golden rules

These eight rules are non-negotiable. Break them and the case becomes unfair, unsolvable, or broken.

1. **One culprit, one closed circle.** A fixed cast; exactly one is guilty. No outsiders wander in.
2. **Motive, Means, Opportunity for *everyone*.** Every suspect has a real motive. The **culprit has all
   three**. Every innocent is **cleared by a discoverable failure** of one - a verified alibi, no access
   to the means, or a motive that collapses on scrutiny. No suspect is filler.
3. **A solvable chain.** The culprit, the key evidence, and the method must all be reachable *from
   authored clues alone*, by examining, questioning, contradicting and combining. No required guessing;
   no clue that lives only in your head.
4. **One fair, foreshadowed twist.** Exactly one central reversal, and at least one earlier clue that
   hints at it. Fair play - never an arbitrary reveal.
5. **Every lie is contradictable.** If a suspect lies, there must be evidence somewhere that breaks the
   lie and moves the case forward.
6. **Red herrings must resolve.** Misleading clues are wonderful, but each one must be explainable by
   something the player can find, so they're never *permanently* misled.
7. **The exam mirrors the solution.** Every exam answer must be deducible from the clues - who, the key
   evidence, the method/motive. Never ask something the player couldn't have worked out.
8. **Technical integrity.** The map connects, every id resolves, every language is complete. (Section 14
   is the full checklist.)

> **Our example, rule by rule.** *One culprit:* Henry Ward. *MMO for all six:* every guest is guilty of
> *something* (see §7), but only Henry has all three. *Solvable chain:* forged note → murder; handwriting
> → which brother; torn file page → a Ward. *One twist:* the mild, overlooked brother did it, not the
> loud obvious one. *Every lie contradictable:* Henry's and Dr. Price's lies each have a piece of evidence
> that breaks them. *Red herrings resolve:* James looks guilty but has an alibi; the doctor has the means
> but only covered a suicide. *Exam mirrors solution:* the three questions ask exactly what the chain
> proves.

## 3. Agatha Christie's playbook

Christie is the patron saint of this genre. Her techniques map almost perfectly onto our mechanics. Use
as many as fit - you don't need all of them, but the starred ones are what stop a case being solvable
"from across the room."

**Setting & cast**
1. **Isolate the stage.** A remote house, a snowbound inn, a cut-off train. Isolation limits the suspect
   pool and traps everyone with the killer.
2. **Trap strangers together.** A cast who don't know whom to trust - paranoia does half your work.

**Suspects**
3. **Typecast into clean archetypes.** Give each suspect a few sharp, legible traits (the grande dame,
   the cad, the fixer). The player thinks they can predict them - that's the setup.
4. ★ **Weaponise expectations.** Turn those archetypes against the reader: the loudest, most obvious
   suspect is a decoy; the least-likely or most-overlooked is guilty.
5. **Ground them in real detail.** Even simple characters feel true when built from one authentic,
   specific habit or line.

**Clues & misdirection**
6. **Lay a fair trail.** Everything needed to solve is present and findable.
7. ★ **The memorable-but-misunderstood clue.** The best clue is one the player *remembers but fails to
   fully grasp* - its true meaning hides in plain sight.
8. **Clues that frame.** A clue that seems to point at one suspect turns out to have been planted to
   implicate them.
9. **Structural misdirection.** Let the situation itself lie - the classic being the helper who's the
   killer.

**Craft**
10. ★ **Keep the culprit fits-several.** Author each clue so it could plausibly implicate two or more
    suspects, so nobody stands out until the chain resolves. This is the single best anti-"solve from
    afar" tool.
11. **Clever, not confusing.** Twisty enough to never be predictable; never so tangled the player quits.
12. **Keep it clear.** Simple names, plain language. Put the difficulty in the *logic*, never in obscure
    wording. Clarity is what makes a puzzle *fair*.

**What NOT to copy.** Christie's typecasting often shaded into ethnic and occupational caricature and
period prejudice. Keep Rule 3 as *archetype and role* - profession, temperament, secret - never as a
stereotype of a group.

> **Our example.** *An Invitation to Judgement* leans on 1 & 2 (snowbound strangers), 3 (six clean
> archetypes), 4 (James the loud decoy, Henry the overlooked killer), 7 (the "suicide note" everyone
> believed for fifteen years but no one checked), 8 (the forged note staged a suicide), 9 (the two
> brothers look alike, so the clue "a Ward did it" mis-points), 10 (every suspect confesses a sin, so
> none stands out), and 12 (plain names - Ward, Price, Cole, Dean, Lane - and clear prose).

## 4. The mechanics, in the player's hands

Before you design, understand what the player actually *does*. Five verbs.

### Examine → a clue
Examining an object reveals its **clue** (its examine and deduce text). Some objects double as
**evidence** - the moment a contradiction rule names their id, they can break a suspect.

> *Example:* examining **Emma's Letter** shows her real, round handwriting - meaningless until set beside
> the forged farewell note.

### Question → a statement
Questioning a suspect gives their current **statement**. Suspects have a state machine - usually
**LIE → TRUTH → PANIC**. A suspect in `LIE` tells a comfortable lie; break it and they flip to `TRUTH`
(or `PANIC`), revealing more.

> *Example:* **Henry Ward** in `LIE`: *"I hardly knew the girl - that was my brother's affair, not
> mine."*

### Contradict → flip a suspect, earn a deduction
This is the heart of the game. A **contradiction rule** lives inside a suspect's state and says: *if the
player presents evidence X, move to state Y and award deduction Z.* The evidence id can be an **object**
or a previously-earned **deduction**.

> *Example:* present the deduction **"the note is in Henry's hand"** to Henry → he flips to `TRUTH`,
> awarding the deduction **`henry_confession`**, with the success line: *"She should have chosen me… and
> Mr. Gray should have let the dead lie."*

Author a lie *and* its contradiction together. If a suspect lies, some clue must break it (golden rule 5).

### Combine → fuse clues into a deduction
A **combine rule** says: *these required ids, together, produce this new deduction.* The inputs are
object ids or earlier deduction ids; the output is a new, uniquely-named deduction that can itself be
used as evidence in a contradiction.

> *Example, three combines:*
> - Farewell Note + Emma's Letter → **`murder_not_suicide`** ("the note is a forgery; she was murdered").
> - Farewell Note + Guest Book → **`note_in_henrys_hand`** ("the forged hand is Henry's, not James's").
> - Mr. Gray's File + Blotter → **`killer_is_a_ward`** ("Gray named a Ward; the killer tore his own page").

Notice how combine and contradict chain: the second combine's output is the exact evidence that breaks
Henry.

### Deduce → spend a token to name a conclusion
Deducing costs an **insight token**. Tokens are scarce, and rank is scored by how few deductions the
player needed - so tokens are both a help and a scoring lever. Combines can also *reward* tokens.

## 5. Designing the mystery (work backwards)

Never design forwards from "here are some clues." Design **backwards from the verdict**:

1. **Decide the ending.** Who is guilty? What was the method? What is the motive? What is the one twist?
2. **Build the chain.** What single deduction proves the culprit? What proves the method? Work back:
   what combine or contradiction yields each, and what clues feed those?
3. **Clear the innocents.** Give each innocent an MMO, then a discoverable reason they're *not* the
   killer (alibi, no means, collapsing motive).
4. **Add red herrings - and their antidotes.** Point some clues at the wrong person; make sure each can
   be explained away.
5. **Foreshadow the twist.** Plant at least one early clue that, in hindsight, pointed at the truth.
6. **Tune difficulty** (below), then write it all in plain language.

### Difficulty tiers

| | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rooms | ~3 | ~5-6 | ~8-10 |
| Suspects | 4-5 | 6-7 | 8-10 |
| Clues | 4-6 | 8-10 | 12-16 |
| Insight tokens | ~2 | ~5-6 | ~8 |
| Contradict / combine | one gentle each | several each | many, chained |
| Twist | 1 small | 1 + a sub-plot | layered (frame-up + hidden identity) |
| Exam questions | 2-3 | 3-4 | 4-5 |

> **Our example** is a textbook **medium**: 6 rooms, 6 suspects, 9 clues, 5 tokens, three combines and
> two contradictions, one twist (the overlooked brother) resting on a sub-plot (the old cover-up).

## 6. Writing the suspects

Six good suspects beat eight thin ones. For each, write down four things:

- **Archetype** - one clear, legible role (the cad, the doctor, the grande dame, the fixer, the maid,
  the quiet brother).
- **MMO** - motive, means, opportunity, and - for innocents - which one *fails* and how the player
  learns it.
- **Secret** - the sin they'll confess when contradicted. In a great case, *everyone* is guilty of
  something, so "who's hiding something?" tells the player nothing (Christie rule 10).
- **Statement & lie** - plain, in-character, short. If they lie, note the evidence that breaks it.

Give the culprit no special spotlight. The most satisfying killer looks *exactly like the others* - or,
better, looks like the one everyone is already blaming.

> **Our six.**
> - **James Ward** - the cad/MP. Loud, arrogant, seduced Emma years ago. *The decoy* - cleared by an
>   alibi (seen by the fire when Gray died).
> - **Henry Ward** - James's quiet, respectable, easily-overlooked brother, who *looks just like him*.
>   *The killer.* He loved Emma, was refused, drowned her, forged the note - and killed Gray to bury it.
> - **Dr. Price** - signed the false suicide verdict for a fee. Has the means; only covered a suicide he
>   believed real.
> - **Lady Cole** - dismissed Emma to protect the family name.
> - **Mrs. Dean** - the maid paid to lie at the inquest.
> - **Mr. Lane** - the solicitor who arranged the hush-money.
>
> The two Wards are the trick: everything points at "a Ward," and the player must tell the loud brother
> from the quiet one.

## 7. Writing the clues

Clues carry the whole puzzle, so write them with care.

- **Ambiguous alone, decisive together.** No single object should shout the answer. Meaning should
  emerge only when clues are *combined* or an alibi is *broken*.
- **Memorable but misunderstood** (Christie rule 7). Give the player a clue they'll remember and
  misread - like a "suicide note" everyone trusts and no one checks against real handwriting.
- **Fits several** (rule 10). Author each clue so more than one suspect could plausibly be implicated.
- **Three layers of text.** Each object has a *description* (what it looks like), an *examine* line (what
  you notice), and a *deduce* line (the conclusion). Keep the deduce line honest but not a giveaway.

> **Our nine clues** are all paper and small objects - a guest book, a blotter, a torn file, a forged
> farewell note, Emma's real letter, an old newspaper, the doctor's notes, love letters, a ledger - and
> none names the killer. The killer's identity only appears when you combine the note with the guest
> book's handwriting, *and* realise the loud brother is alibied. (Art tip: make similar objects look
> physically different - see §13.)

## 8. The final exam

The exam is where the player commits. Its rules are as important as the mystery's, because a badly
built exam lets people brute-force the answer.

- **Every suspect is a live option.** List all suspects (and, for other slots, one plausible option per
  suspect), so no one is excluded and elimination never hands over the answer.
- **Coherent distractors.** Every wrong option must be a *real* thing - a genuine secret, suspect, or
  clue from the case - so each combination tells a believable alternative story.
- **Short, same-shape options.** Uniform length and form, so the correct answer never sticks out.
- **Two slots per question.** Multiple slots multiply the combinations, defeating guesswork.
- **Correct answer placed in the middle** - never first, and not aligned in a pattern across slots.
- **Wording never telegraphs** - no phrasing that reveals the culprit's identity or sex.

> **Our exam** (correct answers marked ✓ - in play they sit scattered in the middle of each slot):
>
> **Q1 - "Mr. Gray was killed by ___, to hide ___."**
> Killer: Lady Cole · Dr. Price · James Ward · **Henry Ward ✓** · Mrs. Dean · Mr. Lane
> Secret: a false verdict · an old affair · **drowning Emma ✓** · casting Emma out · a lie at the inquest · hush-money
> *(Each secret belongs to a real suspect - "Lady Cole, to hide casting Emma out" is a perfectly
> coherent wrong answer, so the player must deduce, not eliminate.)*
>
> **Q2 - "At first the clues accused ___, but the real killer was ___."**
> Decoy: … **James Ward ✓** … | Real killer: … **Henry Ward ✓** …
> *(This question directly tests the two-brothers reversal - a shallow read picks James twice.)*
>
> **Q3 - "Emma Shaw was really ___, and the killer was exposed by ___."**
> Fate: sent away · driven to despair · **murdered ✓** · lost by accident
> Clue: the doctor's notes · **the forged note ✓** · the hush-money ledger · the dismissal record
> *(Each wrong clue really does implicate someone - the doctor, the fixer, Lady Cole - so none is a
> throwaway.)*
>
> The three questions test different things - *who + why*, *the reversal*, *the reframe + proof* - so
> solving one doesn't hand you the rest.

## 9. Watson's hints

Watson is your hint system, and he works in three ways that authors often get wrong. Know exactly what
surfaces where:

- **General hints** - shown by a free `ask watson`. Keep them broad: point at *method* ("start here,"
  "try combining," "distrust the old verdict"), never at the culprit.
- **Contradiction hints** - also shown by `ask watson`, but only once the player is *already holding*
  evidence that would break a suspect. Keep these generic enough to fit *any* of your contradictions,
  and never name the killer.
- **Targeted analysis** - the paid `ask watson <name/thing>`. This reads your **red-herrings** block: if
  you've written a narrative for that suspect or object, Watson says it (this is how you gently clear a
  decoy). Otherwise he gives a neutral "this seems connected / this may be a distraction."

> **Author's note.** There is also a `red_herring` hint *bucket* in the data - it is loaded but **never
> shown**, so don't rely on it. Put your red-herring steering in the `red_herrings` block (for targeted
> asks), not in that bucket.

> **Our example.** General: *"Not everything here is what it was ruled to be - question the old story."*
> Contradiction: *"One of your suspects clings to a story the evidence quietly contradicts; show them the
> proof and watch it crack"* (generic - fits both Henry and Dr. Price). Targeted: `ask watson James Ward`
> and `ask watson Dr. Price` return narratives that clear each of them as red herrings.

## 10. The case file, leads, tasks, ranks

A few supporting pieces round out a case:

- **The case file** is the dossier the player can open: the **victim** (name, relation, notes), an
  **overview** paragraph, and a **profile per suspect** (profession, age, height, weight, relationship
  to the victim, a short bio, and a portrait). Write these in-world; hint at the twist without spoiling
  (our Mr. Gray's note says only that "he was not quite who he seemed").
- **Leads** are optional guided objectives that unlock as the player discovers things (examine the
  scene → trace the crime → unmask the killer). Each becomes visible or complete when a note is
  discovered or a contradiction succeeds.
- **Tasks** are short, deliberately *vague* to-dos ("Search the house," "Question the guests") - they
  orient without spoiling. Keep them ambiguous.
- **Ranking tiers** score the solve by how few deductions were used - fewer is better (Sherlock Holmes),
  more is fine (Dr. Watson), and a default rank always catches everyone else.

## 11. The art

Each case can have its own visual identity, but every image should feel like one set.

**Direction.** Pick a medium and a palette that fit the case's mood. *A Bitter Cup* is warm, candlelit
oil; *An Invitation to Judgement* is a **cold-palette oil** - blue-greys, snow-light at the windows,
sparse warm lamps, dread.

**Framing.** For a whodunit, full-length figures (head to toe) read best and composite into the room
scenes; portrait busts also work for a "cast of suspects" feel. Rooms are full 16:9 scenes; objects are
still-lifes.

**The pipeline.**
1. Write a **shared style block** and a **character continuity sheet** (fixed age/build/dress per
   person) so results stay consistent, and bake the style into every prompt.
2. Generate on a flat **chroma-key background** so you can cut figures/objects out cleanly (green keys
   best against cool-toned figures; blue against warm ones).
3. Cut out, drop the transparent PNGs into the case's image folder, and wire the paths.

**Gotchas we learned the hard way:**
- **Don't let the palette become a filter.** Say the cold is in the *light and mood*, and demand
  "natural, true colours, NO blue tint," or the whole image goes monochrome-blue.
- **Differentiate similar objects.** Three "pieces of paper" render identically - force differences in
  *size, colour, and content* (a small browned scrap vs a large cream folded letter with a red seal vs
  an ink-stained blotting pad).
- **Look-alikes must actually match.** For a two-brothers trick, generate one, keep him as a reference,
  and generate the other *from* that reference.

**Wiring & scaling.** Point each room/object/suspect `imagePath` (and Watson via
`metadata.watsonImagePath`) at the cut-out. Because cut-outs have transparent margins, scale each sprite
from its *opaque* bounding box: a suspect should stand at roughly two-thirds the room's height with feet
near the floor; objects at a sensible clickable size. (In the Case Maker's Placement tab you can do all
of this by dragging.)

> A complete prompt set for our example lives in `docs/AITJ_ART_PROMPTS.md`, and A Bitter Cup's in
> `docs/ABITTERCUP_ART_PROMPTS.md` - read either as a template.

## 12. What makes a case *valid*

Before a case will load, it must pass the validator. Here is the full checklist.

**Required content**
- A `universal_title`.
- A `startingRoom` that exists among the rooms.
- At least one room.
- For each language: a `title`, an `invitation`, a `final_exam` with at least one question, and
  `roomDetails` / `objectDetails` that cover **every** room and object.
- Each suspect has a `homeRoom`, and it resolves to a real room.

**Id integrity**
- Every room neighbour points to a real room, and **all rooms are reachable** from the start.
- Every contradiction `evidenceId` resolves to an object id **or** a deduction id.
- Every combine `requires` id exists (object or deduction), and every combine `resultDeductionId` is
  **globally unique**.
- Every red-herring `recoverable_by` reference exists.
- Every exam question's `correct_combination` names real slots and real choices.

**Consistency & assets**
- If you ship more than one language, every suspect appears in all of them, with identical placement.
- Every `imagePath` either resolves or falls back to a preset (missing art is a warning, not a failure -
  the game never shows a broken image).

**A practical pre-flight**
- [ ] Play your own case start to finish using only the clues - can you actually reach the verdict?
- [ ] Is every exam answer provable from the chain?
- [ ] Is every innocent cleared by something *findable*?
- [ ] Does every lie have a contradiction that breaks it?
- [ ] Do Watson's hints avoid naming the killer?
- [ ] Do the art and object names read clearly and distinctly?

## 13. The JSON, at a glance

A case is one JSON file (bundled in `cases/`, or imported from a case folder). Its skeleton:

```
{
  "universal_title": "...",
  "startingRoom": "...",
  "startingInsightTokens": 5,
  "metadata": { "watsonImagePath": "..." },
  "rooms": [
    { "name": "...", "neighbors": { "north": "..." },
      "objects": [ { "name": "...", "imagePath": "...", "posX": 0.5, "posY": 0.7, "imageScale": 1.2 } ],
      "imagePath": "..." }
  ],
  "combine_logic": [
    { "requires": ["a","b"], "resultDeductionId": "...", "resultText": {"en":"..."}, "tokenReward": 1 }
  ],
  "watson": { "hints": { "general": [ {"id":"...","text":{"en":"..."}} ], "contradiction": [ ... ] } },
  "red_herrings": { "suspects": { "SuspectId": { "is_red_herring": true, "narrative": {"en":"..."} } } },
  "leads": [ { "id":"...", "title":{"en":"..."}, "description":{"en":"..."},
              "visibleWhen":{"type":"ALWAYS"}, "completeWhen":{"type":"NOTE_DISCOVERED","noteId":"..."} } ],
  "case_file": {
    "victim": { "name":"...", "relation_to_case":"...", "notes":{"en":"..."} },
    "overview": {"en":"..."},
    "suspect_profiles": { "SuspectId": { "profession":{"en":"..."}, "age":47, "height_cm":179,
        "weight_kg":80, "relationship_to_victim":{"en":"..."}, "bio":{"en":"..."}, "imagePath":"..." } }
  },
  "localizations": {
    "en": {
      "languageName": "English", "title": "...", "invitation": "...", "description": "...",
      "suspects": [
        { "name":"SuspectId", "displayName":"...", "imagePath":"...", "statement":"...", "clue":"...",
          "initialState":"LIE",
          "states": { "LIE": { "statement":"...", "contradictions":[
                        {"evidenceId":"...","nextState":"TRUTH","rewardDeductionId":"...","successMessage":"..."} ] },
                      "TRUTH": { "statement":"..." } },
          "homeRoom":"...", "posX":0.5, "posY":0.55, "imageScale":2.5, "stationary": true }
      ],
      "roomDetails":   [ { "name":"...", "displayName":"...", "description":"..." } ],
      "objectDetails": [ { "name":"...", "displayName":"...", "description":"...", "examine":"...", "deduce":"..." } ],
      "final_exam": { "questions": [
        { "question_prompt": "... ______, ... ______.",
          "slots": { "slot1": {"slot_id":"slot1","choices":[{"choice_id":"...","choice_text":"..."}]},
                     "slot2": { ... } },
          "correct_combination": { "slot1":"...", "slot2":"..." } } ] },
      "tasks": [ "..." ],
      "rankingTiers": [ { "rankName":"Sherlock Holmes", "maxDeductions":3, "winningStatement":"..." },
                        { "rankName":"Curious Observer", "defaultRank": true, "winningStatement":"..." } ]
    }
  }
}
```

Notes worth remembering:
- **Localized text is a `{ "en": "..." }` map**, so a case can be translated without touching its logic.
- A suspect who never lies needs no `states` - just a `statement` and a `clue` (like an honest witness).
- Object/suspect placement (`posX`/`posY`/`imageScale`, plus flip, non-uniform scale, and rotation) is
  best set visually in the Case Maker's Placement tab, but it all lives here in the file.

## 14. One last word

The best cases in this game share a single quality: **everyone is guilty of something, and only one
thing is murder.** When your suspects all have secrets, your clues all point two ways, your twist was
hiding in plain sight, and your exam refuses to let anyone guess - the player doesn't just find the
answer, they *earn* it.

Now go write one. When you're stuck, reread *An Invitation to Judgement*: a snowbound house, six liars,
a forged note, and two brothers who look the same - and the quiet one did it.

*Happy plotting, detective.*
